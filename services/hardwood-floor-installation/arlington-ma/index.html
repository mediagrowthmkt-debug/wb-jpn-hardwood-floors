Arlington houses and the floors inside them

Arlington was built out over many decades, and the floors show it. In East Arlington the streets are full of two-family homes and bungalows, usually with narrow oak strip that has been covered, patched, or worn thin by renters and owners over the years. Around Arlington Center you find Colonials and Victorians with original wood that is worth keeping, plus kitchens and back rooms that were redone with whatever was popular at the time. Up in Arlington Heights many homes sit on a slope, so one side of the house may have a walkout lower level while the other side is on a crawlspace.

On top of that, Arlington homeowners renovate. Lots tend to be modest, so people build up and out instead of moving: a rear addition, a dormer, a bigger kitchen, a finished basement. Almost every one of those projects ends with the same question for us: how do we make the new floor look like it was always there?

How a JPN installation runs in Arlington

  1. Estimate visit: we measure, test moisture, check how flat the base is, and look at where the new floor will meet the old one.
  2. Written plan: species, width, finish, layout direction, sound control, and transitions, each priced as its own line.
  3. Prep before delivery: if the old carpet, vinyl, or damaged boards are coming out, we remove them and handle the base.
  4. Acclimation: the wood sits in your home until its moisture is close to the subfloor's reading.
  5. Install: boards go down straight and tight, woven into the existing floor where the two meet.
  6. Finishing: sanding and finish coats for site-finished floors, done across old and new together when we are blending.
  7. Wrap-up: trim and thresholds go on, the house gets cleaned, and you walk the floor with us before we call it done.

When new hardwood makes sense in an Arlington home

If the boards you have are sound, refinishing costs less. Installation is usually the better move when:

  • A tenant turnover left carpet or vinyl you never want to see again
  • You are adding on or opening up rooms and need one floor to tie it all together
  • Old strip flooring has been sanded so often the tongue is starting to show
  • A leak around a kitchen, bathroom, or radiator has stained or lifted boards across a room
  • You are finishing the basement and want it to feel like part of the house

Mistakes we see on Arlington installations

When we are called to look at a floor that went wrong, the wood is rarely the cause. The usual problems are:

  • New boards butted against old ones in a straight line at a doorway, so the seam shows forever
  • Solid hardwood laid in a basement or on a slab without a moisture test
  • No thought given to sound in a stacked two-family until the neighbor downstairs complains
  • Wood unboxed and nailed the same day it was delivered
  • A new floor that ends up a little higher than the old one, leaving a lip at every doorway

Quiet floors in East Arlington two-families

Stacked two-family homes are everywhere in East Arlington, and many of them had carpet upstairs for one reason: noise. Swap that carpet for hardwood with nothing else changed, and the people downstairs will hear every chair, every dropped toy, and every late-night walk to the kitchen.

The fix starts below the wood. We screw down loose subfloor panels and deal with squeaks first, because a floor that moves also makes noise. Then, where the floor build allows it, we lay a sound control underlayment made for nail-down or floating hardwood. It will not make a floor silent, but it takes the edge off impact noise, and combined with rugs in the main paths it makes a real difference.

If you own the house and rent one unit, this also protects your relationship with your tenants. If you are a condo owner, check whether your association has a rule about hard floors and underlayment before you pick a product. We are happy to look at those rules with you and build the floor to match.

Joining new wood to original oak in an addition

The most common Arlington installation we do is not a whole house. It is an addition, a dormer, or a back room opened up into the kitchen, where the new floor has to meet narrow oak strip that has been in the house for generations.

The worst way to do it is a straight line across the opening with a metal strip or a wood threshold on top. The way we prefer is to weave the new boards into the old ones: we remove existing boards at staggered lengths along the edge so the new and old interlock, the way the original floor was laid. Then we sand both areas and finish them in the same coats so the color, sheen, and wear read as one floor.

Matching starts with the right wood. Older Arlington floors are usually red oak in a narrow strip, sometimes white oak, and they have darkened with age. We bring samples, sand a small test spot, and talk honestly about how close the match will be before you commit. Sometimes a clean break at a doorway, done on purpose, is the better look, and we will tell you when.

Bringing hardwood up from a finished basement

Arlington lots do not leave much room to build out, so a lot of homeowners build down. A finished basement can become a family room, a guest room, or an office, and wood makes it feel like part of the house instead of a cellar.

Concrete is the catch. A slab can carry moisture even when it looks dry, and solid hardwood laid straight on it will cup or gap. We test the slab first. In most basements we recommend engineered hardwood, which has a real wood surface on a layered core that stays stable, over the right moisture barrier or a subfloor system. For homes in Arlington Heights with a walkout lower level, the same rule applies: test, then choose the build.

We also think about how the basement connects to the rest of the house. If the stairs come down into the new room, we can finish the treads in a color that ties the two levels together, so walking down feels like moving through one home.

Stairs that carry the floor from one level to the next

In many Arlington Colonials and Victorians, the staircase rises right from the front hall, and it is the first thing guests see. A bright new floor at the bottom of a dark, worn, or carpeted staircase looks unfinished, no matter how good the floor is.

We can install new oak treads and risers or sand and refinish the treads you have, then stain them to match the new floor. At the top and bottom we fit stair nosing so the floor flows into the steps instead of stopping short. In a two-family, the shared front stair is often the one that takes the most traffic, and it is worth doing right.

Stairs are priced separately because they are mostly hand work. Refinishing typically runs $40 to $75 per step, and new treads cost more. They show up as their own lines in your estimate so you can decide whether to do them now or later.

Opening the kitchen into the living space

A lot of Arlington remodels take down the wall between a closed kitchen and the dining or living room. The result is great, until you notice that the kitchen had vinyl or tile, the dining room had oak, and now there is a strip of bare subfloor where the wall used to be.

When that happens we usually run one hardwood floor through the whole open space. That can mean new wood in the kitchen woven into the original oak in the dining room, or new wood everywhere if the old floor is too thin or too damaged. We plan the board direction so it reads well in the bigger room, and we handle the patch where the wall stood so you never see where it was.

Wood in a kitchen is a real choice, not a mistake, as long as it is finished well and spills are wiped up. We talk through finish options that hold up to daily cooking and the occasional splash at the sink, and we leave the right gap at the cabinets and appliances so the floor can move with the seasons.

Picking the wood for an Arlington home

Red oak is the floor most Arlington homes were built with, which makes it the easiest choice when you want new rooms to match old ones. White oak has a cooler tone and tighter grain and is popular in additions and remodeled kitchens. Maple, hickory, and walnut change the feel again, from clean and bright to rustic or dark and rich.

Width matters as much as species. Narrow strip, common in older Arlington floors, keeps a Colonial or a bungalow looking like itself. Wider planks show more grain and work well in a new open room, but they can look out of place right next to narrow original strip, so we look at the whole floor before recommending one.

Finish method is the last decision. Prefinished boards go in faster with no sanding dust, which helps in an occupied two-family. Site-finished wood goes down raw and is sanded flat in place, which is the only way to truly blend with an existing floor. Herringbone or chevron in an entry or dining room is possible too, priced as its own line for the extra layout and cuts.

Handling a full season of New England weather

Arlington sits between the Mystic Lakes and the hills, and like the rest of Greater Boston it swings from sticky summers to bone-dry heated winters. Wood takes on moisture in August and gives it up in February. A floor installed without planning for that will cup in summer and open up in winter.

We drop off the wood early so it can adjust to your house, and we do not start until the readings are close. We leave expansion space at every wall and hide it under the baseboard or shoe molding. In older homes with radiators, we pay attention to the floor right around them, where heat dries the wood fastest.

After the install, a few habits keep the floor tight: run a humidifier in the heating months if the house gets very dry, put felt pads under furniture, and keep a mat at the door for winter salt and sand. Small gaps in a cold snap are normal for solid wood and usually close again when the weather warms. If something looks wrong, call us; we are right next door.

Picking a finish sheen for an Arlington home

Sheen is how much the finished floor reflects light, and it changes how a room feels as much as the color does. Matte and satin hide scuffs, dust and pet traffic far better than gloss, which is why most families in Arlington choose one of the two.

Semi-gloss and gloss look sharp in a formal dining room but show every footprint near a back door. We bring finished samples so you can see each sheen on your own floor, in your own light, before anything is decided.

Hardwood at the back door and near the kitchen sink

The spots that wear out first are the ones by the back door, the mudroom and the kitchen sink, where water, sand and snow melt land on the floor every day from November to April.

We plan those areas on purpose: a good mat at the door, a tight seam where wood meets tile, the board ends sealed at the threshold, and a finish with an extra coat in the busiest path. It costs little and adds years before that spot needs attention.

New hardwood floors with dogs and kids

No wood floor is scratch-proof, but some hold up better. Harder species like white oak and hickory dent less, a lower sheen hides fine scratches, and a wire-brushed or textured surface makes daily wear hard to see.

If you have a big dog or small kids, tell us at the estimate. We will point you to the species, finish and texture that make sense for how the house is really used, instead of the floor that only looks good in a showroom.

Caring for a new floor through its first winter

A new floor settles in over its first heating season. Keeping indoor humidity steady, ideally with a humidifier on the heating system, keeps small gaps between boards from opening in January.

Clean with a dry microfiber mop and a cleaner made for finished wood, never a steam mop or a wet mop left standing. Put felt pads under chairs and table legs before the furniture goes back, and wait until the finish has cured before rugs go down.

Frequently asked questions

What does it cost to install hardwood in an Arlington two-family unit?
A typical unit floor of 800 to 1,000 square feet lands around $6,400 to $14,000 for prefinished hardwood with materials and labor, or about $8,000 to $16,000 for site-finished solid wood. Removing carpet, fixing the subfloor, adding sound control underlayment, or redoing the stairs adds to that. We measure on site and list every item in writing before you decide.
Can new hardwood in my addition match the original oak in the rest of the house?
In most Arlington homes, yes. We match the species and strip width, weave the new boards into the old at the opening, and then sand and finish both areas together so the color reads as one floor. We will tell you on site how close a match is realistic for your wood.
Will a new hardwood floor make my upstairs unit louder for the tenants below?
It does not have to. Hard floors carry footsteps, so in a two-family we put a sound control underlayment under the new wood where the design allows it, fix squeaks in the subfloor, and recommend rugs in the main walking paths. Ask us about it during the estimate so it is built into the plan.
How long does a hardwood installation take in Arlington?
A few rooms of prefinished wood usually go in over a few days. Site-finished floors add days for sanding, stain, finish coats, and cure time. A full unit or an addition tied into existing floors takes longer, and your estimate comes with a schedule.
Can we stay in our Arlington home during the installation?
Most families do. We close off the work area, protect the rest of the house, and contain dust. With site-finished floors you will need to stay off those rooms during finish and cure, and in a two-family we plan the work so the other unit can keep using the shared entry.
Is it better to refinish my old oak or install new hardwood?
If the boards are solid and have enough wood above the tongue, refinishing usually costs less and keeps the original character. If they are worn thin, water damaged, or missing in rooms you are adding, new installation is the better call. We often do both in the same house and finish them together.
Do you install hardwood for landlords between tenants?
Yes. We work with owners of two-families and small buildings in Arlington who need a unit floor done between leases. Tell us the move-in date, and we will build the schedule and the product choice around it.
