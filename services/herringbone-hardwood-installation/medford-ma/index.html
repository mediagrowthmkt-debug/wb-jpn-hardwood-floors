Herringbone or chevron?

The two patterns are often confused. In herringbone, rectangular boards meet at a 90-degree angle, forming the classic broken zig-zag. In chevron, the ends of the boards are cut on an angle so they meet in a continuous, seamless V. Both are stunning; chevron reads a touch more formal and modern, herringbone a touch more traditional. We install both and can show you samples of each in your own space.

Why the pattern demands a perfect base

A straight-lay floor can hide small subfloor imperfections; a pattern floor cannot. Every angle in a herringbone floor multiplies any error, so a base that is even slightly out of flat shows up as gaps and misaligned points across the whole room. We prep and level the subfloor to a dead-flat plane before a single board goes down, which is the single most important reason a herringbone floor looks right for decades instead of drifting out of pattern.

Planning the layout so the pattern is balanced

The difference between a herringbone floor that looks custom and one that looks off is almost always the layout. Where the pattern starts and how it is centered decides whether the room feels balanced or awkward.

We plan the layout before laying a board, typically centering the pattern on the room and running it toward the main sightline or light source, so the points march evenly across the space and the cuts at the walls are balanced side to side rather than a sliver on one edge.

In an older Medford home with out-of-square walls, which is most of them, this planning is even more important. We work the pattern so it reads straight and intentional to the eye even when the room itself is not perfectly square.

Solid vs engineered for a herringbone floor

Herringbone comes in both solid and engineered hardwood, and the right choice depends on where it is going, exactly as with a straight-lay floor.

Solid herringbone on a main level over a wood subfloor is the classic, and it can be refinished over its life. Engineered herringbone is the smart pick over a concrete slab, in a basement, or with radiant heat, because its stable core keeps the tight pattern from moving as humidity changes.

We help you choose based on the room and subfloor, so your Medford herringbone floor stays tight and true rather than opening up at the points a couple of winters in.

Finishing a pattern floor

How a herringbone floor is finished changes how the pattern reads. A site-finished floor gives a seamless surface with no micro-bevels between boards, so the pattern flows as one continuous plane, ideal for a showpiece floor.

Prefinished herringbone goes down faster and is walkable sooner, with a durable factory finish, though the slight bevels between boards are visible up close.

We talk you through the trade-off and, for site-finished floors, can apply a custom stain that makes the grain and the geometry of the pattern really stand out, turning the floor into the centerpiece of the room.

Choosing board size for the right scale

The size of the boards changes how a herringbone floor reads as much as the species or color does, and matching the scale to the room is part of planning the pattern well.

Smaller, narrower pieces create a busier, more traditional herringbone that suits a period Medford entry or study, while larger, longer boards make a bolder, more contemporary pattern that opens up a bigger room. Get the scale wrong and the pattern can feel cramped in a large space or overwhelming in a small one.

We look at the size and proportions of your room and recommend a board size that keeps the pattern balanced, so the finished floor feels designed for that specific space rather than dropped in from a showroom.

Borders, transitions, and framing the pattern

A herringbone floor lands best when the edges are handled with as much care as the field. Where the pattern meets the walls and doorways, a clean border frames it and hides the cut ends, giving the floor a finished, intentional look.

We plan borders and transitions as part of the layout, whether that is a simple straight border that frames the pattern or a more detailed inlay, and we make sure the pattern meets adjoining rooms and thresholds cleanly rather than trailing off into awkward slivers.

In the open floor plans and connected rooms common in renovated Medford homes, these transitions are what make a herringbone floor feel like part of the architecture instead of an isolated rug of wood.

Caring for a herringbone floor

A herringbone floor is cared for like any hardwood, but because it is a showpiece, keeping it looking its best is worth a little attention. The finish protects the wood, so protecting the finish is the whole game.

Felt pads under furniture, mats at entries to catch grit and winter salt, prompt wipe-ups of spills, and cleaning with a hardwood-appropriate product rather than water keep the pattern looking sharp. Grit is the main enemy of any wood floor, and a pattern floor shows wear paths just as a straight floor does.

Down the road, a site-finished herringbone floor can be screened and recoated, or fully refinished if it is solid wood, to bring the pattern back to like-new, protecting the investment you made in the craftsmanship.

Where herringbone works best in a home

Herringbone makes the strongest impression in the rooms people gather and arrive in. It is a floor that rewards being seen, so placing it where it will be noticed gets the most from the investment.

Entries and foyers are classic choices, greeting everyone who walks in; dining rooms and formal living rooms wear it beautifully; and in open-plan Medford renovations, a herringbone kitchen or great-room floor becomes the anchor of the whole space. It also suits studies and primary bedrooms where you want a touch of craftsmanship.

We help you decide where a pattern floor will earn its keep and where a straight-lay floor makes more sense, so your budget goes to the rooms where herringbone truly lands rather than being spread thin.

Why experience matters more on pattern floors

With a straight-lay floor, a competent installer gets a good result. With herringbone, the gap between an experienced crew and an inexperienced one is dramatic and permanent, because every angle either lines up or it does not, across the entire room.

Small errors that would vanish in a straight floor compound in a pattern: a fraction of a degree off on each board becomes a visibly drifting pattern by the far wall. There is no hiding it and no easy fix after the fact. This is precisely why so many flooring companies either avoid herringbone or subcontract it out.

We do this work ourselves, and we plan, prep, and lay the pattern with the care it demands. When you are paying for a showpiece floor, the crew's experience is not a detail, it is the whole difference between a floor that looks custom and one that looks off.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between herringbone and chevron?
In herringbone the boards meet at a 90-degree angle, forming a broken zig-zag. In chevron the ends are cut on an angle so they meet in a continuous V. We install both and can show you samples of each.
Does herringbone cost more than a standard floor?
Yes, a bit. It takes more layout, more cuts, and more skilled labor to keep the pattern perfect. You get a clear, documented estimate up front, with no surprises.
Can I get herringbone in engineered wood?
Absolutely. Herringbone comes in both solid and engineered hardwood. Engineered is a great fit over concrete or radiant heat, and we help you choose the right one for your Medford home.
Can you install herringbone in an older Medford home?
Yes, and it often suits them beautifully. The key is a dead-flat, sound subfloor; we prep and level the base first so the pattern stays true in a century-old house.
How long does a herringbone floor take to install?
Longer than a straight-lay floor because of the layout and the many precise cuts. We give you a firm schedule in the estimate; the leveling and layout stages are where we invest the time to get the pattern right.
Can herringbone go over radiant heat?
Yes, using an engineered herringbone product rated for radiant heat. Its stable core keeps the tight pattern from moving as the system cycles, which solid wood would not tolerate as well.
