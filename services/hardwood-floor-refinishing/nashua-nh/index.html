Built for the floors Nashua actually has

Much of Nashua was built out in subdivisions between the 1970s and the 1990s, and many of those Colonials and split-entries were finished with 2 1/4 inch red oak in the living areas and halls. After thirty or forty years, that oak usually has plenty of wood left. The finish is what wears out: gray paths from the front door, scratches by the kitchen table, and an orange tone that dates the house. In the North End and near downtown, the older wood homes often have floors that have been sanded before, sometimes more than once, and those need a more careful plan. We look at each floor and set the sanding approach to match it.

Refinish first, replace only when you have to

If your boards are solid hardwood and still sound, refinishing almost always costs much less than ripping them out. It keeps the original floor, avoids a dumpster in the driveway, and takes days rather than weeks. When a section is too damaged to save, we replace those boards with matching wood and refinish the room as one surface.

Our Nashua refinishing process

  1. Estimate visit: we measure each room, check how much wood is left, look at the subfloor and any water damage, and send you a line-by-line estimate.
  2. Scheduling: since we travel from Medford, we lock in your start date and the number of days ahead of time.
  3. Prep: furniture moves out of the rooms, doorways and the kitchen are sealed, and vents are covered to keep dust where it belongs.
  4. Sanding: dust-contained machines take the floor down to bare wood through the full grit sequence, with edges and corners done by hand.
  5. Repairs: we fill gaps, fasten loose boards, and replace any damaged boards with matching wood.
  6. Color and finish: you approve a stain sample on your own floor, then we apply two or three coats of finish with a buff between them.
  7. Final walkthrough: we tell you when to walk on it, when furniture and rugs can return, and we look at the finished floor together.

Signs it is time to refinish your Nashua floors

Most floors tell you when the finish has given up. These are the signs we see most in Nashua homes:

  • Worn, gray lanes from the garage door or front entry into the kitchen
  • Scratches from dogs, chairs, and furniture that make the floor look tired even after cleaning
  • Cloudy or white spots near the sink, dishwasher, and entry doors where water sat on the finish
  • Gaps that open wide every winter and a few boards that squeak or feel loose
  • An old yellow or orange finish that no longer suits your kitchen, trim, or paint

What to expect while we work

Most single floors take two to four working days plus cure time. Bigger homes or a full color change take longer, and your estimate tells you how many days to plan for.

Because we come up from Medford, we confirm your start date ahead of time and keep the crew on your job until it is done.

We seal off the work area and collect the sanding dust as we go, so the rest of the house stays livable.

Mistakes Nashua homeowners should avoid when hiring a refinisher

A good-looking quote can still lead to a bad floor. Here is what to watch for:

  • No site visit before the price: a real quote needs someone to measure and look at the wood.
  • No dust containment: open-bag sanding leaves fine dust on every surface in the house.
  • Vague wording: the estimate should say how many coats, which finish, what repairs, and who cleans up.
  • Rushing the color: choosing stain from a store chip instead of testing it on your own floor.
  • Sanding too hard on an older, thinner floor: once the wood is gone, it cannot be put back.

Refinishing oak in Nashua's 1970s to 1990s homes

A big part of Nashua is made up of homes built from the 1970s through the 1990s, and many of them share the same floor: red oak strip, usually 2 1/4 inches wide, in the living room, dining room, and halls, sometimes running into the kitchen. These floors were often finished with a product that has slowly turned amber over the years, which is why so many of them now look orange.

The good news is that this oak usually has a lot of life left. It may never have been sanded, or only once. Under the worn finish, the wood is typically solid and flat. A full sand takes away the old color, the scratches, and the gray traffic lanes, and lets you decide on a new look from scratch.

Many Nashua owners use refinishing as part of updating the whole house. A floor in a natural tone or a soft brown can make a 1980s Colonial feel current without touching the layout. If you are also painting, replacing kitchen cabinets, or putting in new stair treads, we help you plan the order of work so the floor is done last and stays protected.

Older floors in the North End and around downtown

The North End and the streets around downtown and Main Street have many older wood-frame houses, and their floors often carry a long history. It is common to find oak in the main rooms and softer wood in back rooms or upstairs, patches where walls moved, and boards that have been sanded several times over the decades.

Before we sand an older floor, we check how much wood remains above the tongue and groove. If the floor is thin, we use a lighter sanding approach or recommend a screen-and-recoat. If it has plenty of wood, we refinish it fully and blend the patches so the room looks like one floor.

Older homes also show more seasonal movement and wider gaps. We fill the gaps that will stay filled, leave seasonal gaps that would only crack the filler out by summer, and set loose boards so they stop squeaking.

New Hampshire winters, river humidity, and your floor

Southern New Hampshire winters are long and cold, and heating systems dry the indoor air for months. Hardwood shrinks in that dry air, so gaps between boards open up, sometimes enough to catch crumbs and dust. When humid summer weather comes back, the boards swell and the gaps close again.

Nashua sits where the Nashua River meets the Merrimack, and summers here can be humid. A floor that takes on too much moisture, from a damp basement, a leak, or a wet entry, can start to cup, with board edges higher than the middle. We check the moisture before sanding, because sanding a cupped floor while it is still wet can leave it crowned once it dries out.

The best protection is steady indoor humidity: a humidifier in the dry months and air conditioning or a dehumidifier in summer. Fixing the source of any moisture problem before refinishing keeps the new finish looking good for much longer.

Choosing between oil-based and water-based finish

Finish decides how your floor looks, how long it takes to be usable, and how it wears. We use both oil-based and water-based finishes and help you choose based on how your home works day to day.

Water-based finish dries fast, has a low smell, and keeps oak close to its natural color without the amber shift. It suits families with kids or pets, people who want to stay home during the job, and anyone after a lighter, cleaner look. Today's commercial water-based finishes are very tough.

Oil-based finish gives oak a warm amber tone that deepens with age. It takes longer to dry and smells more while it cures, so some households plan a night or two away. People with older homes often like how it looks next to original woodwork.

We also talk through sheen. A matte or satin sheen hides dust, scratches, and footprints better than semi-gloss, which is why most families pick one of those.

How to pick a stain color you will still like in five years

Stain looks different on every floor. Red oak takes color differently from white oak or pine, and a north-facing room changes how a color reads compared with a bright kitchen. Choosing from a small sample at a store is the most common cause of regret.

After sanding, we brush several samples right onto your floor. You look at them in morning light and at night with your lights on, next to your paint and trim, before you decide. We do not move ahead until you approve the color.

In Nashua's newer homes, many owners move from orange toward natural, light brown, or warm gray-brown tones. In older homes, people often choose a medium brown that works with original doors and trim. A clear coat with no stain is also a great option when the grain is attractive on its own.

Refinish or replace: an honest look at the numbers

One of the most common questions we get from Nashua homeowners is whether it is cheaper to refinish a wood floor or replace it. For a solid floor in sound shape, refinishing wins by a wide margin. You pay for sanding, repairs, and finish, not for removing the old floor, buying new wood, and installing it.

Replacement starts to make sense when the wood has been sanded too thin, when large areas have deep water or pet damage, or when the subfloor underneath needs real work. Even then, sometimes only part of the floor needs to be replaced and the rest can be refinished to match.

Because JPN is a full-service flooring company, we can do either one. That means we have no reason to push you toward the more expensive option. We look at your floor, explain what we see, and put both paths on paper if you want to compare them.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to refinish 1000 sq ft of hardwood floors?
Using our typical range of $3 to $5 per square foot, a standard refinish of 1,000 square feet comes to roughly $3,000 to $5,000. With a custom stain, the range is about $4,000 to $7,000. Repairs and stairs are added on top, and your written estimate gives the exact figure for your floor.
Is it cheaper to refinish a wood floor or replace it?
If the boards are solid and have wood left above the tongue, refinishing is much cheaper than replacing. Replacement means removing the old floor, buying new wood, installing it, and then finishing it. We check your floor on site and tell you plainly which option makes sense.
Does JPN really come to Nashua from Massachusetts?
Yes. We are based in Medford, MA, and we travel to southern New Hampshire for refinishing work. We set the date with you in advance and bring the full crew and equipment, so the job runs from start to finish without waiting on anything.
Can we stay in the house during the work?
Most families can. The rooms being refinished stay closed until the finish is safe to walk on, and we seal off the rest of the house. If you choose an oil-based finish, some people prefer to be away for a night or two because of the smell.
How long does refinishing take in a typical Nashua home?
Most single floors take two to four working days, plus cure time before furniture and rugs go back. A whole house or a big color change takes longer. Your estimate lists the number of days, and we confirm the start date in advance.
Can you refinish a floor that has been sanded before?
Often, yes. We check how much wood is left above the tongue and groove. If there is enough, we refinish it. If it is thin, we suggest a lighter sanding plan, a screen-and-recoat, or a repair, and we tell you why.
Will refinishing get rid of pet stains and deep scratches?
Surface scratches sand out completely. Deep gouges and dark pet stains that soaked into the oak usually do not, so we cut out those boards and weave in matching wood before the finish goes down. We point out each spot during the estimate visit and include the repair in the price, so you know exactly what will disappear and what gets replaced.
Do you refinish stairs too?
Yes. Stairs are priced separately, usually $40 to $75 per step, because most of the work is done by hand. We can match the stairs to the color of your refinished floor.
