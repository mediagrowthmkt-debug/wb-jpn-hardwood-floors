The buildings behind most Malden floors

A large share of Malden's housing went up in the early twentieth century: two-family homes with one unit stacked over the other, triple-deckers with three matching apartments, and Victorians scattered through the older neighborhoods. Most of them were built with oak strip floors in the front rooms and sometimes softer wood in the kitchens and back bedrooms. A century of tenants has usually put several sandings and a few layers of patching on top of that wood.

Those floors are worth saving. Oak strip refinishes well, it costs far less than tearing out and replacing a whole apartment, and a freshly refinished floor is one of the first things a prospective tenant or buyer notices when they walk in.

Owner-occupied two-family homes

Plenty of Malden owners live in one unit and rent the other. That makes the job a little more personal: you want your own floor done right, and you want the tenant's floor done without disturbing the tenant more than necessary. We plan the work so each unit has its own schedule, we keep the dust out of shared stairwells and hallways, and we tell both households in advance which days will be noisy.

A common plan is to do the owner unit when you can step away for a few days, and the rental unit at the next turnover, using the same stain and sheen so the building stays consistent. If you would rather do both at once, we sequence them so the stairs and front door stay usable the whole time.

How a Malden unit gets refinished

  1. Walk-through and estimate: we visit the unit, check the wear layer and any damage, and send a written estimate per unit or per building.
  2. Schedule around the lease: we set the start date from the move-out and work back from the move-in date so cure time is built in.
  3. Seal off the work area: the unit is emptied of loose items, doorways and vents are covered, and shared stairwells are protected.
  4. Sand or screen: worn floors are sanded to bare wood through the full grit sequence, and lightly worn floors get a screen-and-recoat.
  5. Repairs: squeaks are set, gaps are filled, and stained or broken boards are replaced with matching wood.
  6. Finish coats: two or three coats of finish go down with a buff between them, chosen for durability in a rental.
  7. Handoff: we walk the unit with you or your property manager and share the video, so the unit can be listed with confidence.

When a Malden floor is due for refinishing

Rental floors show wear faster than most, and catching it early saves money. These are the signs we look for:

  • A dull, gray traffic path from the entry to the kitchen and bathroom
  • Furniture drag marks and scratches left behind after a move-out
  • Dark spots near radiators, windows, or where a plant or pet bowl sat
  • Old finish that has worn through in doorways so bare wood is showing
  • Photos of the unit that make the floors look tired next to other listings

What to expect on the job

An empty unit is the easiest job. Most single-unit refinishing takes two to four working days plus cure time, depending on square footage and the finish you choose. A screen-and-recoat can be quicker.

If you live in the unit, we work room by room where possible and keep a path open to the bathroom and kitchen. With a low-odor water-based finish, many owner-occupants stay home through the job.

In a building with other tenants, we keep the noise to working hours, seal the dust inside the unit, and clean shared areas at the end of each day.

Mistakes Malden landlords and owners should avoid

A cheap refinish that fails in a year costs more than doing it right once. Watch for these:

  • Doing a full sand every turnover when a screen-and-recoat would have been enough, which wastes wood the floor cannot get back
  • Waiting until the finish is worn to bare wood, which turns a quick recoat into a full refinish
  • Choosing a high-gloss finish for a rental, which shows every scratch from the next move-in
  • Booking the floors after the painters, so drips and tape marks have to be fixed again
  • Hiring a crew without insurance to work inside a building where other people live

Refinishing on a tenant turnover schedule

For a rental owner, the real cost of a floor job is not only the invoice. It is the days the unit sits empty. So in Malden we start every turnover job by asking two dates: when the old tenant leaves and when the new one moves in.

With those dates, we build the schedule backward. Cure time comes first, because a new tenant dragging a couch across a finish that has not cured will undo the work. Then the finish coats, then the sanding and repairs, then setup. If the window is too tight for a full refinish, we tell you, and we suggest a screen-and-recoat that fits.

The best order for a turnover is usually cleaning out, minor repairs and painting, then floors last. That way the new finish is the last thing to go down and nothing gets dripped on it.

Many Malden leases turn over around the same time of year, which makes those weeks busy for every trade. If you know a unit will turn over, call us when notice is given rather than the day the keys come back. That gives us time to see the floor, quote it, and hold the dates.

Screen-and-recoat between leases

A screen-and-recoat is the most useful tool a Malden landlord has. Instead of sanding to bare wood, we lightly abrade the existing finish and apply a fresh coat. It removes the dull, scuffed look, costs less than a full refinish, and is usually faster.

It works when the damage is in the finish, not in the wood. Light scuffs, a dull traffic path, and minor scratches come out well. Deep gouges, dark stains, and bare spots do not, and those need a full sand.

The real advantage is long term. Every full refinish removes a little wood, and floors in hundred-year-old buildings have already lost some. Recoating every few leases keeps the finish healthy and saves the remaining wood for the times a full sand is truly needed.

One caution: if the floor was ever waxed or treated with an oil soap or shine product, a recoat may not bond. We test for that before we recommend it.

Picking a finish that holds up in a rental

Rental floors get moved across, dragged on, and cleaned with whatever is under the sink. The finish needs to handle that. A commercial-grade water-based finish is often the best fit: it dries fast, has low odor for neighbors, stays clear, and is very durable.

Oil-based finish is also tough and gives oak a warmer amber look, but it takes longer to cure and smells for a few days, which is harder in a building where other people live.

Sheen matters as much as the product. Satin or matte hides scratches and dust between cleanings, while gloss shows everything. For most Malden rentals we recommend satin.

If you want a stain color, a medium or natural tone hides wear better than very dark or very light floors, and it photographs well for listings.

Old floors in two-families and triple-deckers

Floors in buildings this age have usually been sanded before, sometimes more than once. Before we quote a full refinish, we check how much wood is left above the tongue. If a floor is close to the limit, we say so and recommend a recoat or repairs rather than sanding through it.

It is also common to find patched areas where a wall, a radiator, or an old kitchen layout once was. We weave in matching boards so the patch disappears under the new finish instead of standing out.

Kitchens and back rooms sometimes have softer wood or later flooring over the original. We look at each room separately and tell you which floors are worth refinishing and which would be better replaced.

Older buildings also move with the seasons. When the heat runs in winter, the air dries out and small gaps open between boards, then close again in the humid summer. A little seasonal gapping is normal in a building this age. We fill what should be filled, leave the rest alone, and explain the difference so you are not calling a contractor every February.

Squeaks are another thing tenants complain about in stacked units, because every step carries to the apartment below. Where we can reach the problem from above, we fasten the loose boards before sanding, which quiets the floor and makes the new finish last longer.

Back stairs, front halls and shared spaces

In many Malden two-families and triple-deckers, the front hall and staircase are shared by every household. They take the most traffic in the building and are often the first thing a visitor sees.

Refinishing shared stairs takes planning, because people still need to get in and out. We can do the treads in stages, half the width at a time, or schedule the stair work for a day when tenants are notified and have another way out. Stairs are priced per step, usually $40 to $75 depending on condition.

A refinished front hall and stairway makes the whole building feel better kept, which helps with both tenant retention and resale.

When refinishing helps you sell or refinance

If you are getting ready to sell a Malden two-family or triple-decker, or to have it appraised, refinished floors are one of the most visible improvements for the money. Buyers walk through every unit, and worn floors make a building look like it needs more work than it does.

Refinishing all the units at once also gives the building a consistent look, which photographs well for listings. If budget is limited, we can do the owner unit and common areas fully and recoat the rented units.

Our written estimate and recorded video make it easy to show a buyer or appraiser exactly what was done and when.

Caring for refinished floors in a rental

Give the floor time to cure before move-in, and tell new tenants that the finish is fresh. Felt pads on furniture legs prevent most scratches. A welcome mat at each entry catches sand and winter salt.

Include simple floor care in your move-in sheet: damp mop only, a cleaner made for finished wood, no steam mops, no oil soaps, and no wax. Those products cause most of the damage we see between tenants and can stop a future recoat from bonding.

With that care and a recoat every few leases, a Malden floor can go many years before it needs another full sand.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to refinish the floors in a Malden apartment?
Standard refinishing usually runs $3 to $5 per square foot, and a custom stain brings it to about $4 to $7. A typical unit's price depends on how many rooms have wood and how much repair the floor needs. We measure on site and send a written estimate for each unit.
Can you refinish a unit between tenants?
Yes, this is a big part of our Malden work. Tell us the move-out and move-in dates, and we plan the sanding, finish, and cure time to fit inside that window so the unit is ready to show.
Will the tenants in the other units be affected?
Some noise during sanding is unavoidable, but we use dust-contained equipment and seal off the unit and shared hallways. We give you the noisy days ahead of time so you can let the other households know.
Is a full refinish always needed between leases?
No. If the finish is worn but the wood is not scratched deep or stained, a screen-and-recoat is faster and cheaper and still makes the unit look cared for. We tell you on site which one the floor actually needs.
Do you work with property management companies?
Yes. We work with managers, contractors, and investors on single units and whole buildings, with estimates broken out per unit and firm dates that fit your turnover schedule.
Can I stay in my unit while you refinish it?
Often yes, especially with a water-based finish. We work in an order that keeps your kitchen and bathroom reachable, and we tell you which rooms are off limits on which days.
Can you refinish only the shared stairs and hallway?
Yes. We can refinish common stairs and halls on their own, in stages if needed, so every household keeps a way in and out of the building.
