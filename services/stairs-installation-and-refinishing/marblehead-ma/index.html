Old staircases, tight spaces

Many Old Town houses were built with stairs that are steeper and narrower than anything a builder puts in today. Some turn at the top or bottom, some run straight up against a wall, and most leave very little room to work. Large machines do not fit, so stair refinishing here is mostly hand work: small sanders, scrapers, and a lot of time on each tread. We plan for that from the start, and we price it honestly per step instead of pretending a staircase is a flat floor.

Stairs and the floor they connect to

A staircase never stands alone. It meets a front hall at the bottom and a landing or upstairs hallway at the top, and the eye reads them together. When only the floors get refinished, the stairs look tired next to them. When only the stairs are done, the floors look worn. We often do the stairs and the hallway floor in the same job, so color, sheen, and edges line up from the entry to the second floor.

When new stair installation makes more sense

Some staircases are past refinishing. Treads split along the grain, nosings broken off, steps sanded so many times they flex underfoot, or a renovation that opens up the stairwell and calls for a fresh start. In those cases we install new hardwood treads and risers, either over the existing structure or as part of a rebuild with your builder. New treads are cut to fit each step, including the odd angles of winders and the out-of-square walls that are normal in an older house, then sanded, stained, and finished on site so they match your floors. You get the look of a staircase that belongs to the house, with the strength of new wood.

How we refinish and rebuild stairs

  1. Visit and estimate: we look at every tread, riser, nosing, and railing, note squeaks and loose steps, and write a per-step estimate.
  2. Protection: the hall floor, walls, railings, and nearby rooms are covered, and the stairwell is sealed for dust.
  3. Repairs first: loose treads are secured and squeaks are quieted so the finished stairs are solid underfoot.
  4. Stripping and sanding: old finish and paint come off, and every tread and nosing is sanded by hand and machine.
  5. Color test: the stain is tried on a tread and checked against your hallway floor before we commit.
  6. Finish and paint: treads get two or three coats of durable finish, and risers are painted or stained as planned.
  7. Walkthrough: we check every edge with you, step by step, and clean up before handing the stairs back.

Signs your Marblehead staircase needs work

Stairs take the same footstep in the same spot every day, so they show wear before the floors do. It is time to look at them when:

  • The center of each tread is worn through the finish or worn into a visible dip
  • Risers have chipped, scuffed, or yellowed paint from years of shoes
  • A step creaks, rocks, or feels soft when you put your weight on it
  • The stairs no longer match the floors you have already refinished
  • Carpet runners have left staples, tack holes, and an uneven color line on the treads

What to expect during stair work

Most staircases take a few days, with drying time between coats. Humid coastal weather can slow drying, and we plan for that instead of rushing.

If the stairs are the only way upstairs, we work in a way that keeps a path open and tell you every evening which steps you can use.

Dust stays inside the work area, and we protect the walls, railings, and floors around the stairwell. When we leave, the hallway is clean.

Mistakes to avoid when hiring for stairs in Marblehead

Shortcuts show more on stairs than anywhere else in the house. Watch out for:

  • A quote by the square foot: stairs are hand work and should be priced per step.
  • Finishing over loose or squeaky treads: the noise stays and the finish cracks at the joints.
  • Sanding thin old treads too hard: worn pine steps may need new treads, not more sanding.
  • Messy lines between stained treads and painted risers, which you will see every day.
  • A glossy, slick finish on a steep staircase where good footing matters most.

Steep and winding staircases in historic homes

Older Marblehead houses often have stairs that would never be built today: tall steps, shallow treads, and turns at the top or bottom where the steps fan out in a wedge shape. These winder steps are narrow at one end and wide at the other, and each one is a slightly different shape.

That shape makes them hard to sand and harder to finish evenly. We work each winder by hand, follow the grain, and keep the nosing edge crisp along its full length. On very worn steps we check thickness first, because a tread that has been walked on for two hundred years may not have much wood left.

If a step is cracked or too thin to sand safely, we replace just that tread with matching wood and blend it into the run, so you keep the original staircase and gain a solid step.

Stained treads with white risers

Wood treads with white risers is a classic look for a reason. It brightens a narrow stairwell, shows off the wood where your feet land, and fits both antique homes and newer ones. It is also one of the most requested stair jobs we do on the North Shore.

The work is in the details. We sand and stain the treads, apply the finish, then mask and paint the risers so the line where tread meets riser is clean and straight. Skirt boards and the trim along the wall usually get painted to match the risers, which ties the whole staircase together.

Risers take scuffs from shoes, so we use a durable paint that cleans up well. If you want the stairs to match your hall floor, we choose the tread stain with the floor in view, and often finish the hallway in the same job so both sides of the staircase line up.

Paint and finish need their own drying time, and on the coast damp air can stretch that out. We plan the order of coats so the risers are painted after the tread finish has set, which keeps the line sharp and keeps paint off the wood. That patience is what makes the finished staircase look clean from the bottom step and the top landing alike.

Painted stairs and what is under the paint

Many old staircases in town have been painted several times over the years, sometimes treads included. Under the paint there is often good hardwood or old pine that can be brought back, but not always.

Before we promise a stained wood look, we open a small test area to see the wood, the condition of the old fills and patches, and how deep the paint has soaked in. Pine in particular can hold paint in its grain, and some treads will look better painted again than stained.

We show you what we find and give you options: strip and stain, strip and repaint with a fresh finish on top, or new treads where the old ones are not worth saving. You choose with clear information, not a guess.

Stripping old paint from stairs is slow, careful work. Paint sits in corners, along the skirt boards, and in the small gaps where tread meets riser, and it all has to come out cleanly before stain can go on. We price that work up front as its own line, so you see what the stripping costs and can decide if a painted finish is the better value for some parts of the staircase.

Squeaks, loose treads, and coastal moisture

Stairs squeak when parts rub against each other: a tread moving on its riser, a nail working loose, or a joint that has opened. In a coastal house, seasonal moisture makes this worse, because wood swells in damp months and shrinks in dry ones, which loosens joints over time.

We fix squeaks before we finish anything. Depending on how the stairs are built and what we can reach, that can mean securing treads from above with hidden fasteners, adding glue and blocks from below when the underside is open, or tightening the joint between tread and riser.

Fixing the structure first matters. Finish over a moving tread cracks at the edges in a season, and you end up refinishing again. A quiet, solid staircase also simply feels better to walk on every day.

Carpeted stairs to hardwood

Plenty of Marblehead staircases were carpeted at some point, often to hide wear or add grip on a steep run. Pulling that carpet is one of the most satisfying jobs we do, but what is underneath varies a lot.

Sometimes there are solid hardwood treads that just need sanding, nail and staple holes filled, and a fresh finish. Sometimes the treads were never meant to be seen, made of rough lumber or plywood, and the right answer is new hardwood treads and risers installed over the existing structure.

We pull the carpet, check every step, and give you a clear plan and price for refinishing or new treads, matched to the floors around the stairs. Hardwood stairs are easier to keep clean too, which helps in a house where sand and salt come in from outside.

Safe footing on steep stairs

On a steep staircase, finish is partly a safety choice. A high-gloss finish can feel slick in socks and shows every scuff, while a lower sheen gives you better grip and hides wear. We talk through sheen with you before the first coat goes on.

Edges matter just as much. A crisp, even nosing is easier to see and easier to land on, especially at the top of a steep run or in dim light. That is why we spend so much time on nosings and why we refinish them by hand.

If your stairs had a runner for grip, we can discuss a refinished staircase with a new runner installed after the finish cures, so you get the wood look on the edges and better footing in the center.

Doing the stairs and the hallway floor together

The stairs and the hall floor below and above them are seen in one view. Refinishing them in the same job is usually the best way to get a color and sheen that match, because the same stain, the same finish, and the same crew are used across both.

It also saves time for you. One schedule, one setup, and one round of protection for the house, instead of two separate projects months apart. We plan the order so the stairs and the floor dry without anyone needing to cross wet finish.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to refinish stairs in Marblehead?
Refinishing existing treads and risers usually runs about $40 to $75 per step. Stripping old paint, repairing loose treads, or refinishing the railing adds to that. After we see your staircase, we give you a written per-step estimate with every item listed.
Can you do stained treads with white risers?
Yes, it is one of the most common requests we get. We sand and stain the treads, finish them, and paint the risers with clean, straight lines where the two meet. It works well in older houses because it lightens a narrow stairwell and shows off the wood.
Can you refinish the steep, narrow stairs in an old Old Town house?
Yes. Tight and steep staircases take more hand work, since big machines do not fit, but they refinish well. We check each tread for wear and thickness and tell you which steps can be sanded and which may need new treads.
How long will I be without my stairs?
Most staircases take a few days including drying time between coats. If your house has only one staircase, we plan the work so you can still get up and down, for example by finishing every other step first, and we tell you each day which steps are safe to use.
Can you replace just one or two worn treads?
Yes. If most of the staircase is sound, we replace only the treads that are cracked or too thin, using matching wood, and blend them in with the stain and finish so the run looks consistent.
Can you convert my carpeted stairs to hardwood?
Yes. We remove the carpet, look at what is underneath, and either refinish the existing treads or install new hardwood treads and risers matched to your floors.
Do you refinish the railing and balusters too?
We can. Railings, newel posts, and balusters are priced on top of the per-step cost, and we either match them to the treads or paint them to match the risers, depending on the look you want.
Do you travel to Marblehead for stair jobs?
Yes. JPN is based in Medford and travels to Marblehead and the rest of the North Shore. We come out to see the staircase, send a written per-step estimate, and then book the job on a schedule that works for your household.
